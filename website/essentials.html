Before you climb: the quantum world

At a glance

You do not need to understand quantum physics to learn quantum computing. This page exists because most people want a mental picture anyway, and it is uncomfortable to keep using a word you have never had explained.

The short version: quantum mechanics describes the very small, where a system need not have settled on one answer yet. A quantum computer is a machine that keeps a set of such unsettled possibilities, steers them so the good ones reinforce and the bad ones cancel, and then looks — getting exactly one answer back.

This course teaches the steering. Not the hardware, not the philosophy — the algorithmic layer in the middle.

🏔️ Feel free to skip this page. You do not need plate tectonics to climb a mountain, and you do not need quantum physics to build quantum circuits. Nothing in the six basecamps assumes you read this. It is here for the people who cannot comfortably proceed without a picture — and if that is you, you are in the majority.

What “quantum” actually means

Quantum mechanics is the theory that describes the smallest things there are — electrons, atoms, photons of light. It is not a different universe with different rules; it is a refinement of the physics you met at school. For a football or a coffee mug, Newton's classical physics and quantum mechanics agree so closely that nobody bothers with the harder one. Shrink to the scale of a nanometre and the classical description simply stops being accurate.

One piece of jargon is worth having, because the rest of the course leans on it. Physicists say state to mean a complete description of a system at one moment — where everything is, how fast it is moving, which way it points. For a classical computer the state of some memory is easy to write down: you list the bits, say 11010, and you are finished.

The interesting part is that a quantum system's state can be something a list of bits cannot express.

Four things that surprise everyone

These four words come up in every quantum conversation you will ever have. Each one gets a full basecamp later; here is just enough to orient you.

1 · Superposition

A qubit need not have settled on 0 or 1. Instead its state carries a number for each possible outcome — an amplitude — and those numbers say how strongly each outcome is in play. Crucially this is not a statement about your ignorance. It is not that the qubit secretly holds a value you have not looked up. Before measurement there is no value to look up, and that difference has been tested in laboratories rather than merely argued about.

→ Basecamp 1 builds this from a spinning coin, with a widget.

2 · Measurement

Look at a qubit and it commits: you get one plain 0 or 1, at random, with odds fixed by those amplitudes. The unsettled state is gone the instant you look. This is why a quantum computer with a huge number of possibilities in play cannot simply hand you all of them — you get one answer per run, every time. Nearly every exaggerated claim about quantum computing is a claim that forgets this.

→ Basecamp 1, and dissected on the Myth Autopsy.

3 · Gates

Gates are the moves. Each one is a small, precise, reversible change to the state of one or two qubits — the quantum cousins of the AND and OR gates inside a laptop, plus several with no classical counterpart at all. A list of gates, in order, is a circuit, and a circuit is what a quantum program actually is. They are drawn left to right, rather like sheet music.

→ Basecamps 2 and 3, where you build them by clicking.

4 · Entanglement

Two qubits can share a single joint state that cannot be pulled apart into “this one is doing X, that one is doing Y”. Measure one and you learn about the other immediately, however far apart they are — which sounds like a telephone and is not one. No signal travels, and no message can be sent this way. You will get to try it and fail, which teaches it better than a paragraph can.

→ Basecamp 3, and myth #2 on the Myth Autopsy.

A decoder ring for the phrase you will meet everywhere

Sooner or later — probably today — you will read that “a qubit is 0 and 1 at the same time.” It is the most repeated sentence in popular quantum computing, and you will find it in genuinely good textbooks and courses, not only in bad journalism. We do not use it here, and it is worth thirty seconds explaining why, so that you can read everyone else without getting confused.

How to translate it

The phrase is reaching for something true: both amplitudes are genuinely there, and both of them matter to what happens next. That is not a figure of speech. It is the reason interference works, and interference is the entire source of quantum computing's power. Keep that half.

The misleading half is the word values. There are not two values sitting side by side waiting for you to pick one. There is no value yet at all.

So: both amplitudes are there. What is not there is a value.

Read the popular phrase as shorthand for “you must keep track of both possibilities” and it becomes harmless — that is exactly what careful writers mean by it. Read it as “it is secretly both answers at once” and you will be stuck when you meet measurement (why does it give only one?) and stuck again at interference (how can two answers cancel?). Almost every learner gets snagged on this once. Getting snagged is normal; staying snagged is what we are trying to prevent.

What a quantum computer physically is

It helps to know what the machine looks like, if only to stop imagining something mystical. There are several competing technologies — trapped ions, neutral atoms, photonics, silicon spin qubits — but the most familiar today are superconducting qubits, the approach used by IBM, Google, Rigetti and a number of university labs.

The quantum part is a chip, not unlike the one in your laptop, on which the qubits are tiny superconducting circuits. It only behaves itself when cooled to around ten millikelvin — roughly a hundredth of a degree above absolute zero, colder than deep space. That is why the iconic photograph of a quantum computer is not of a computer at all: it is the gold dilution refrigerator that keeps the chip cold, cooling in stages down through the layers, with control wiring running the length of it.

Around it sit racks of thoroughly classical electronics generating microwave pulses and reading the faint signals back, drawing power on the order of a domestic fast-charger. Only a fingernail of the whole apparatus is quantum. The rest is refrigeration and signal processing — and the software that drives it is written on an ordinary laptop.

Today's machines are somewhere around where classical computers were in the 1940s: room-sized, loud, power-hungry, and needing experts on hand. That is not a criticism. It is a stage.

Three layers — and which one this course is

“Quantum computing” names at least three different jobs, done by different people with different training. Knowing which one you are studying saves a great deal of confusion about what you are supposed to already know.

  1. The physics and hardware layer Building qubits that hold still: materials science, microwave engineering, cryogenics, error correction. Deeply physical, and where most of the money currently goes.
  2. 🏔️ The algorithmic layer — you are here Given working qubits, what do you do with them? Encode a problem, choose the gates, arrange the interference so the right answer becomes the likely one, and measure honestly. This is the part that is mostly mathematics and code, the part you can practise today on a simulator, and the part that decides whether a quantum computer is ever worth switching on.
  3. The applications layer Chemistry, optimisation, finance, machine learning: deciding which real problems have the shape a quantum algorithm can exploit — and, just as often, showing that they do not.

Quantum Ascent lives in the middle. You will not build a qubit here, and you will not be asked to derive quantum mechanics from postulates. You will take working qubits as given and learn to steer them — finishing by pointing a real algorithm at a real optimisation problem and scoring the result honestly.

That middle layer is also the most portable thing to learn. Hardware will change underneath you; several of today's leading platforms may not be the ones that win. The reasoning — amplitudes, interference, cost functions, approximation ratios — carries across all of them.

What quantum computers are not good at

A short list, because a resource that only sells is not worth trusting.

They are not storage. You cannot pack a library into a few qubits and read it back. Measurement returns one answer per run, so retrieving n bits of classical data still costs you n qubits' worth of measurement. Quantum memory is a poor hard drive.

They are not simply “faster computers”. More qubits without carefully arranged interference is a bigger haystack, not a faster search. Qubit counts in headlines are close to meaningless on their own.

They are not ready. Today's machines are noisy, and useful quantum advantage has been demonstrated for very few problems — not including Max-Cut, the problem you will solve at this course's summit. You will meet that fact head-on rather than at the end in small print.

If you are confused, that is the correct reaction

Quantum mechanics is genuinely counter-intuitive. People who have studied it for decades still argue about what it means, and several of the sharpest physicists of the twentieth century got parts of it wrong in public and in print. If this page left you with a rough picture and a few unanswered questions, that is exactly the right state to begin in.

The good news is that the functionality is far more approachable than the philosophy. You can learn to build circuits that work, predict what they will do, and understand why, without ever settling what a wavefunction really is. That is what the next eight hours are for — and unlike this page, all of it is hands-on.
